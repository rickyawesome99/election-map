LEGISLATIVE ATLAS
One page per chamber, one record per district

The chamber pages (/states/[state]/legislature/house and /senate, Nebraska's single chamber under senate) show every state legislative district: its members, every general election since 2016 with named candidates, its presidential vote on each map it has existed on, its demographics and its redistricting history. A district is addressed by hash (#hd-31, #sd-5), so a link from search or the district finder opens the page on that seat. Nothing here is a model: this tab documents the three datasets the pages are built from and the measured error of the one that is estimated.

Scope
General elections only. Special elections are not collected, and a seat whose only race in a year was a special concurrent with the general shows its votes without names.
District keys
The boundary data's own codes: numbers almost everywhere, letters for the Alaska Senate, county code plus number in New Hampshire (BE1), names in Vermont and Massachusetts. A past year's row is the race held under that key on that year's map, which can be a different place: Ohio renumbered its whole House in 2022, so HD-31's 2020 row is a Cincinnati seat and its 2022 row a Summit County one. The lines column and the redistricting section say so.
Downstream
The corrected chamber margin is live in the State TPL; the district projection and the State Legislatures board are documented below.
DATASET A
President 2016, 2020 and 2024 on every map era

For every chamber, the presidential vote inside each district on the current map AND on each superseded map in public/state-leg-districts-historical/. 50 states, 220 chamber-eras. The 2024 figure on the current map is the precinct-exact one from the legislature page; everything else is an overlay.

METHOD

Dave's Redistricting publishes each state's 2016, 2020 and (for 38 states) 2024 presidential returns disaggregated to census blocks and re-aggregated to 2020 VTDs, with geometry. Each VTD is intersected with the era's district polygons in an equal-area projection and its votes split by the share of its area in each district; slivers under 0.2% are dropped and the rest renormalised so every VTD is allocated in full. Per-district rounding is largest-remainder per party, so a chamber's sum reproduces the state total exactly. The 2020 VTDs are used for the 2011-cycle maps too, since no 2010-VTD geometry is published.

MEASURED ERROR
the overlay's 2024 margin vs the precinct-exact 2024 file, per district, current map only

Median mean absolute error across the 75 chambers with an independent 2024 answer: 0.04 points of margin. The error is almost entirely where a district line cuts a large rural VTD unevenly in population, so the worst chambers are rural Houses. DATASET B
Candidates, incumbency and winners

Names, party, votes and, where the source carries them, incumbency and the winner, attached to the per-district results. Live coverage: 28,772 of 28,808 district-years named (99.9%), 28,769 with a marked winner, 21,609 with a marked incumbent, 10,168 unopposed, 721 with no published count.

SOURCES, IN ORDER OF PRECEDENCE
2016–2022
Klarner's State Legislative Election Returns candidate file: one row per candidate per contest, with exper = inc for the incumbent and outcome = w for the winner. Regular generals only (etype g; Louisiana's jungle rounds, with the runoff winning where one was held). Names: the file's surname field runs compound surnames together and sometimes glues on a middle name or another candidate's surname ("Siffordthayn" for Bill Sifford, who ran against Steven Thayn), so where it marks a surname as compound the name as filed and the file's own part types decide — a known hyphenated or compound surname is shown with its hyphen or space; a merely possible one only where another source spells the same person with one; an extra word glued on is dropped for the filed surname.
2024
MEDSL precinct returns (names only) for 75 chamber-years; the Wikipedia chamber-election articles for Arizona, Iowa, Oregon, Nebraska and New Mexico; the New York State Board of Elections canvass, kept per fusion line so the list matches the party-line vote buckets.
Odd years
Klarner through 2021; the Wikipedia articles for Louisiana, Mississippi, New Jersey and Virginia 2023 and New Jersey and Virginia 2025; the Mississippi recapitulation sheet and the Louisiana Secretary of State workbooks where the articles fell short.
Hand-transcribed years
New Hampshire 2022 House (the state's election-database export, which also corrected the earlier county-PDF parse) and Vermont 2018 Senate (the election archive's town export).
Derived flags
Where a source marked no winners, incumbents or unopposed seats in a chamber-year, they are derived: winners by name-match to the sitting member elected that year (which resolves Idaho, Washington and West Virginia post races), then top-N by votes; incumbents by name-match to the chamber's winners in the previous four years; unopposed where no more real candidates than seats. A derived flag never sits beside a sourced one in the same chamber-year.
INVARIANT

A candidate list is written only when its votes sum to the district's recorded total (exactly for the official sources, within 0.6% for Wikipedia's transcriptions). No vote number was changed by any of these passes, with one exception recorded in the change log: New Hampshire's 2022 House, where the official export showed the earlier parse had double-counted 17 districts.

DATASET C
Redistricting history and the crosswalk

For each pair of consecutive maps, what every new district is made of: 8,530 district-transitions, 7,058 of them with changed lines.

METHOD

The two maps' polygons are intersected and each piece gets its 2020 Census population by area-apportioning the same VTD file's counts, so a new district's composition is measured in people rather than land. The record carries the previous-map districts it draws from with their population shares, its county composition on both maps, and its 2020 presidential margin on both sets of lines from dataset A. The description is generated from those numbers, geography first and the lean shift as a number, in the style of the House redistricting descriptions.

The contributor shares are also the crosswalk the 2026 projection will use: a seat's pre-redraw results are relocated onto today's lines by population overlap and corrected by the presidential delta between the two polygons.

DATASET D
The 2026 field

Every district on the 2026 ballot with its general-election candidates, from Ballotpedia's chamber election pages (fetched 2026-10-10): 94 chambers, 5,668 districts, 3,830 with both major parties, 1,066 with no incumbent running. Ballotpedia's own caveat applies: the lists may be incomplete until every state certifies its ballot. Special elections on the same November ballot, which the chamber pages leave out, are added from data-entry/state-leg-specials-2026.csv (Ballotpedia's special-election list and each district page, checked against Wikipedia): the seat is up in 2026 — the whole district, or in a multi-member district the one member's seat, run as its own race — and a special for the weeks left of a term whose regular race is on the same ballot is skipped. Nebraska's candidates carry their party from data-entry/nebraska_2026_candidate_parties.csv.

METHOD

Each page's “general election 2026” candidate table is read: one row per district with Democratic, Republican and Other columns, “(i)” after an incumbent, “(N seats)” on a multi-member district and a party or write-in tag on the Other entries. District rows are matched to the boundary data's keys the same way the results are; unmatched rows are reported, never guessed. A seat counts as contested when both major parties have a non-write-in candidate, and as open when nobody in the field is marked an incumbent.

WHY A SEAT IS OPEN
Wikipedia chamber articles, fetched 2026-10-10

The English Wikipedia article on each chamber's 2026 election keeps a list of members not seeking re-election with the reason (scripts/fetch-state-leg-outgoing-2026.py): 968 outgoing members across 81 chambers (362 retiring, 246 running for other office, 203 term-limited, 113 lost primary, 29 not running, 15 resigned). The articles write these three ways (district first, ordinal first, or name first with “representing district N”); each line is parsed to a district key, a name, a party (from the subsection or inline) and the reason as written. Chambers whose article carries the status only in a results table (North Dakota, West Virginia Senate) or not at all are absent. Shown on the district header (“Not running”) and on the member's candidate page.

DATASET E
Fundraising

Each general-election candidate's total contributions for the cycle, per the state's own filings: 50 states, 540 chamber-cycles (2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026), 33,255 district-cycles, 57,382 of 65,802 listed candidates matched to a filing. Shown on the district header (the current cycle, with its data-through date) and as a Raised column in the electoral history, and the input to the projection's money term (below).

State portals
For the states Transparency USA does not cover, each state's own disclosure system, one script per state (scripts/fetch-state-leg-finance-{st}.py): race or committee totals where the portal publishes them (Oklahoma, Nebraska, Kentucky, Tennessee, Massachusetts, Vermont, Maine, North Dakota, New Jersey's post-election summary), the sum of itemized contributions elsewhere. Maryland's 2022 cycle starts in November 2020, so it undercounts; Hawaii and Missouri use report totals. Oregon's ORESTAR blocks a crawler after a few hundred searches, so its data is read through the Oregon Campaign Finance mirror (orestar-dashboard.vercel.app), a daily copy of every ORESTAR transaction export since 2006: a committee's contributions over the two calendar years to the election, the committee placed on a district when its office names one that cycle's candidate and otherwise matched by full name statewide.
2016 and older cycles
Transparency USA starts with 2018, so the 2016 cycle (Virginia's 2017) of its states comes from the same kind of state records, one script each, and so do Kansas's 2016 House (the Ethics Commission's published summary), North Dakota's 2016 (its statements then printed only contributions over $200, so that is the 2016 figure for every candidate), West Virginia's 2016 (paper filers stay blank) and South Dakota (filing PDFs; state law bars commercial use). Maine 2016, Maryland 2018 and Ohio 2016 come from The Accountability Project's public copies of the old state exports (Ohio's: contributions, fundraiser and other income and in-kind, Transparency USA's definition, which it reproduces within 1% for 174 of 202 candidates in 2018; its office and district columns are the candidate's current ones, so Ohio matches by name). Where candidates filed on paper the scanned reports were read by eye: West Virginia 2016 (the election-year-to-date box) and New Hampshire 2016 (the last report's campaign-to-date receipts less any surplus carried in; New Hampshire's e-filers come from its transaction export). Idaho's 2018–2022 come from the state instead of Transparency USA (whose files held the 2026 race pages, a crawl error, and which now refuses requests): 2018 from the Secretary of State's archived contribution file, which reproduces the reports' calendar-year-to-date totals; 2020 and 2022 from the Sunshine portal, every donation including unitemized and in-kind, less returns, without loans or credit-card line items (with loans it reproduces the year-end reports' totals for 61 of 65 candidates checked), plus 2019 from each committee's archived annual report. The portal records a candidate's latest office and district, not the race's, so a record is placed in a district only where the race's own map gives it one, and otherwise matched by full name. Arkansas's 2018–2022 legacy filings are listed by registration year, usually the odd year before the election, so both years are read. Mississippi (2019 and 2023, four-year terms) sums the term's four calendar years from the Secretary of State's portal: e-filers' typed reports, and for paper filers the calendar-year-to-date box, read by OCR or by eye and kept when the form's own arithmetic holds, or, where too many boxes are blank to check it, when nothing on the form contradicts the year-to-date figure (a part larger than the total, a balance that does not close); a candidate with any year unread is blank. Kansas 2016 Senate and 2018–2026 come from the Secretary of State's campaign finance viewer: each receipts-and-expenditures report's contributions plus in-kind, summed over the reports whose periods fall in the cycle's two years. Paper reports are read by OCR or by eye and count when the summary's own arithmetic holds. The filings' printed periods are often wrong (stale forms, an e-filing system that printed post-election reports as the whole year), so periods are checked against the cash on hand each report carries forward from the one before: a report is placed where its cash chain puts it, and a candidate with a report that cannot be placed, a hole between reports the cash does not bridge, or two same-date versions of one report more than 10% and $250 apart is blank.
Not used
Stanford DIME's public recipients file lists state legislative candidates but withholds their receipt totals (the NIMSP-sourced figures), and its 2024 coverage is California and Ohio only. OpenSecrets' state profiles (the NIMSP data, from October 2026) cover every state but sit behind a Cloudflare check that needs a headed browser, under a non-commercial licence; declined.
JOIN

A filing is matched to the atlas's candidate for that district and year (the 2026 field, or the result's names) by name: the full name, then first and last name with nicknames canonicalised (“Mike” is “Michael”), then a first initial and surname within the same party, then the surname alone when it is unique on both sides. A state-portal record arrives already matched (scripts/assign-state-leg-finance.ts, which also reads committee names such as “Parrish for NC House”) and carries the candidate it was matched to, so it joins on that. A filing that matches nobody is kept as unmatched and shown; a candidate with no filing is null, never zero. The two-party money gap is (R − D) / (R + D) over the matched Democratic and Republican candidates, undefined when either side is missing.

THE MODEL
The 2026 district projection

Every seat on the 2026 ballot (6,163 seats, 94 chambers, 5 in play) is projected from the atlas's own data and simulated chamber by chamber on the State Legislatures board. The environment is the forecast's fitted E (today D+4.7, σ 2.9).

LEAN
row NM   = margin − INCUMBENCY_PTS × incumbent sign − β*(state) × E(year) − money(race)       seat rows: the district's general in each year it was contested
           margin − β*(state) × E(year) + PRES_GAP_K × gap(chamber)                     presidential rows on today's lines (2016, 2020, 2024)
gap      = Σ_years 2^i × mean_seats [seat-row NM − the seat's mean presidential NM] / Σ 2^i      years with PRES_GAP_MIN_SEATS seat rows on today's lines, i rising toward the latest
           capped at the latest year's own gap (no larger, same sign, else 0): a habit is carried only as strong as it last was
seat margin (multi-member, unequal slates) = (R̄ − D̄) / (R̄ + D̄) × (R + D) / total      R̄, D̄ the average nominee's votes; equal slates: (R − D) / total
relocate = Σ share_i × margin_i / coverage  +  [pres(today) − Σ share_i × pres_i / coverage]   a race on a superseded map, through the crosswalk
lean     = Σ_years YEAR_WEIGHT × coverage × [ Σ_types TYPE_WEIGHT × mean NM ] / Σ YEAR_WEIGHT × coverage     two-pass Huber on seat rows
           in the latest year with a seat row, that row's TYPE_WEIGHT × RECENT_SEAT_BOOST (L 0.2 → 0.6: half of that year's lean)
share_i is the population share of today's district that was in old district i (dataset C); coverage is the share covered by contested old districts, and a row below MIN_COVERAGE is dropped. The presidential delta is taken in the presidential year nearest the race. money(race) is the race's money term at its full-cycle receipts (below), averaged over a relocated row's old districts like its margin; taking it out keeps one cycle's lopsided fundraising from passing into the district's lean. The gap is the chamber's down-ballot habit: where voters split their tickets the same way year after year (Vermont's Republicans run about 16 points ahead of their presidential nominee, West Virginia's Democrats about 10, down from 34 in 2016, which is why the gap is capped at its latest value), presidential rows alone would misplace every seat, and most of all the ones with no contested race of their own to correct them. In a multi-member district where one party ran fewer nominees than the other, summed votes hand the fuller slate a margin no seat is decided on (two Democrats and one Republican for two seats is about double the Democratic votes); the average nominee against the average nominee is the measure the seat is decided on, and the backtest scores against it.
FORWARD
margin = lean + β*(state) × E(2026) + INCUMBENCY_PTS × incumbent sign + money(2026)      incumbent sign from the 2026 field's marked incumbent
σ²     = SIGMA² + STATE_SHOCK² + (β* × σ_E)²        P(D) = Φ(−margin / σ)
chamber: 5,000 draws of a shared national shock N(0, σ_E) × β*, a shared chamber shock N(0, STATE_SHOCK), and N(0, SIGMA) per seat; seats not up hold their member; a field with one major party is decided unless a credible independent is on it (below)
headline seats = each seat's projected winner, summed (the chamber map's count); the 80% range and the control odds come from the draws
decided seats: in a multi-member district a party keeps max(0, seats − the other party's nominees); a post (Idaho's A/B, Washington's 1/2) with one party's nominee is that party's; only the remaining seats are drawn
Multi-member districts draw each seat left after the decided ones on the district's margin. A nominee on both major lines (Oregon and Vermont cross-nominate, New York fuses) is one person, in the party they sit for — or, for a newcomer, the party holding the seat — and a past race with one person on both lines is no D-vs-R contest and contributes no seat row. The chamber shock is what makes a chamber's seats move together beyond the national swing: a state-wide miss (a governor's race on the same ballot, a state issue, a redistricting the lean has not caught up with) lands on every seat at once, so chamber ranges are wider than independent seats would give. A seat with no listed field has no projection, and a chamber whose unprojected seats could decide control reports no control odds. Nebraska's ballot is nonpartisan: each candidate is placed in their party (registration, or a state party's endorsement where the two differ — data-entry/nebraska_2026_candidate_parties.csv), the caucus they would sit with, and its past races are read the same way; a candidate with neither stays an independent.

Presidential rows against the seat's own races, renormalised over the types present in a year — the District TPL's President/House ratio. The sweep below finds the holdout flat between a 0.25 and a 0.40 seat share once the latest race is boosted, so the designed ratio is kept.

FIXED PRIOR
MONEY

Fundraising enters the way it does in the federal forecast: as the part of a race's money gap that its situation does not already explain. An incumbent, or a party running in friendly territory, usually out-raises the other side, and the lean and incumbency terms already count that; only the money beyond the expected gap moves the margin. The expected gap is fitted on 17,562 contested races with both sides' money on file (2016 2234, 2017 102, 2018 2878, 2019 144, 2020 2791, 2021 127, 2022 2876, 2023 158, 2024 2909, 2025 102, 2026 3241): an incumbent's side raises 30.9 points of gap more, each point of presidential margin adds 0.97, R² 0.50. It uses no election result, so the backtest fits it on every year without leaking an outcome, and it is refitted from whatever money is on file each time the site is built: a state or cycle added to dataset E joins the fit and the term with no other step.

gap%        = (R − D) / (R + D) × 100      the two sides' cycle contributions (dataset E)
expected%   = a(year) + b × incumbent sign + c × presidential margin on that year's lines      least squares, every chamber pooled
money(race) = clamp(MONEY_K × (scale × gap% − expected%), ±MONEY_CAP)      scale = PARTIAL_CYCLE_GAP_SCALE for 2026, 1 for a past race
either side's money missing → 0: the typical gap for the race's situation (the lean and incumbency already carry it), not "no money"
A year's own intercept absorbs a cycle's party-wide money tilt (and 2026's partial level). Multi-member districts sum each party's nominees. The strip on past seat rows uses the same term at full-cycle receipts. Districts the money data does not reach yet carry no money term, so as coverage fills, more seats get one.

0.03
Points of margin per point of money gap beyond the expected one: the sweep's best cell in both holdout years when set (2026-10-18); since the slate margin and down-ballot gap, 0.04 edges it by 0.002, within noise, so it is kept.

INDEPENDENTS

A ballot with one major party is usually decided, but not where an established independent is the real opponent. 16 such races are modeled this cycle (7 sitting independents, 4 with a strong last race, 5 backed by a major party), and 5 seats have no major party on the ballot and go to an independent outright.

credible   = sitting  |  ≥ IND_RECORD_SHARE in their last head-to-head in the seat  |  backed by the absent party (data/stateLegIndependents.ts)
advantage  = ± (lean + β*(state) × E(2026))  +  INCUMBENCY_PTS × (inc_I − inc_opp)  +  personal       sign: the independent takes the absent party's place
personal   = (Σ_last two residual + IND_SHRINK_K × prior) / (n + IND_SHRINK_K)      prior = IND_PRIOR_PTS, or 0 for a backed newcomer
residual   = past advantage − [± (lean + β* × E(year)) + INCUMBENCY_PTS × (inc_I − inc_opp)]      head-to-heads against the same party, on today's lines
P(I)       = Φ(advantage / √(σ² + IND_SIGMA²))
A party that backs an independent over its own nominee (Alaska's Senate S, where the Democrats list Bryce Edgmon above their nominee and ranked choice transfers the nominee's votes) puts the independent in its place. An appointed incumbent counts as sitting but gets no incumbency, the federal forecast's rule. In a multi-member district where the party fields a nominee for every seat (Vermont's Chittenden-15), the independent is racing that party's last nominee inside one electorate: lean and environment drop out, only incumbency and noise remain, and the other seats go to the party. Independents on a ballot with both major parties and no backing are not modeled.

THE MODEL
The 2026 chamber popular vote

Each seat on the ballot gets a projected vote count, split between the parties by its district projection, and the chamber's seats add up two ways: raw, the aggregate the certified results will print, and adjusted, with every seat that lacks a major party imputed as the contested race it would have been — the rule the corrected past chamber margins apply with the presidential vote, here applied with the projection. Today: 174.1M votes over 5,660 districts in 88 chambers, 1,951 of them imputed in the adjusted figure. Both numbers head every chamber page beside the corrected chamber margin.

VOTES
level     = the state's 2026 top-of-the-ticket votes from the turnout estimate (/analysis/internal-methodology/turnout: CVAP × blended midterm rates, ticket and competitiveness terms)
share_d   = the district's share of the chamber's 2024 presidential vote on today's lines (dataset A)
factor_d  = votes cast in the seat's race per seat per expected top-race vote: leg votes ÷ [seats × top votes(year) × share_d(that cycle's presidential election)]
            measured in each basis midterm from the seat's own contested race; on a superseded map, the population-weighted mean of the old districts (dataset C, coverage ≥ MIN_COVERAGE)
            blended on BASIS_WEIGHTS, shrunk toward the chamber's contested mean: (pres votes_d × own + DROPOFF_SHRINK_VOTES × mean) / (pres votes_d + DROPOFF_SHRINK_VOTES)
V_d       = level × share_d × factor_d × seats up      × UNOPPOSED_TURNOUT for a printed unopposed race or two nominees of one party; 0 where the state prints no count (FL, HI, OK, TX)
A multi-member district's votes are candidate votes, as the certified aggregates are: each voter casts several, so a chamber like Arizona's House or New Hampshire's runs past the ballots cast, and its factor says so. A seat with no contested race in either basis midterm takes the chamber mean per seat. The level is the turnout estimate's, so its range (each basis midterm alone) carries through to the chamber total.
SPLIT AND SUMS
contested    R = (100 − other + margin) / 2 · D = (100 − other − margin) / 2       margin = the district projection's (lean + β*·E + incumbency), points of the full vote
other        the chamber's other-party share over its last two elections in races of the same kind (a minor candidate on the ballot or not; one party with a minor candidate), shrunk toward the national figure with OTHER_SHRINK_RACES pseudo-races
one party    all but the other share to it; a modeled independent takes the absent party's place on their projected advantage
multi-member each seat's share of the district's votes on its own contest: a post with one party, a guaranteed seat, the rest on the margin
raw          Σ counted districts                 adjusted = Σ every seat as a contested two-party race: V_d at contested turnout, the projection's margin, the chamber's no-minor-candidate other share
The adjusted figure is the chamber's preference, not its ballot: Massachusetts prints D+58 in a House where 120 seats have no Republican, and the imputed sum lands near its lean. A seat with no lean (no presidential figure on its lines) stays as its raw race in both sums; Nebraska's nonpartisan votes are summed by each candidate's party.

CHANGE
Forecast locked as final (8:30 AM ET, November 3)

FORECAST_FINAL_AT in lib/forecastClock.ts pins the forecast clock at 2026-11-03 13:30 UTC. Every date-dependent input reads that instant instead of the wall clock: poll aging, the race, generic-ballot and approval averages, house effects, the environment's horizon, the Overview, the forecast-over-time series (it ends on November 3) and the daily snapshot. Without the lock the numbers would keep drifting through the day on poll aging alone: a nine-hour later run moves 36 races by 0.1 points and Senate control by 0.1 points. The locked numbers are data/forecast-history/2026-11-03.json. `npm run check-lock` (also a CI step) rebuilds the snapshot and fails on any difference, so a later data or code change cannot silently move the final forecast. Every forecast page carries a "Final forecast" pill (components/FinalForecastPill.tsx): race pages, the Senate / House / Governor forecast pages, the Overview, state pages and the legislature pages.

A larger legislative environment term: tested, not adopted; the gap to State Navigate and ElectIndex is deliberate

Our seat margins run about 3.4 points more Republican than State Navigate's and ElectIndex's across every rating band and state, so the environment term was put to the holdout. The live term is β*(state) × E(2026). E is the forecast's national environment: a generic ballot of D+8.1 becomes a House vote of D+7.4 (half the historical poll miss added back), and the line E = 0.53 + 0.56 × House vote fitted on 2016–2024 maps that to E = −3.6. On the same seats, that already puts the 2026 projection D+6.8 from the 2024 legislative results, about the size of the 2016→2018 swing (D+6.5 on seats contested both times, on unchanged maps, against a 9.7-point swing in the House vote). The test multiplied the term by λ: on the forward term only, on the forward term and the past seat rows' strip (legislative seats respond λ× to E in every year), or on every row. It was scored on 2022 and 2024 as before, and on 2018 and 2020, whose results ran on superseded maps and were relocated onto today's lines through the crosswalk. Unlike the standard holdout, the mean bias was left in the error, since a uniform environment miss is what is being tested. Two target environments were used: E(Y) as fitted from that year's statewide results, and E as the live chain would have read it from the final generic ballot.

EFFECT With the fitted environment, the best λ is 1.0–1.2 in every year, and no year gains more than 0.06 of MAE. With the generic-ballot environment, the best λ follows each year's poll miss rather than any stable legislative response: about 1.6 in 2018 (polls under-read the wave; bias at λ 1 is 1.9 toward D), under 0.8 in 2020 (polls overstated Democrats; bias 3.1 toward R), 0.8 in 2022 and 2.0 in 2024. State legislative projection: a money term, in the forecast and in the district lean

Fundraising (dataset E) now enters the 2026 district projection the way it enters the federal forecast: money(race) = clamp(MONEY_K × (scale × gap% − expected gap%), ±MONEY_CAP), gap% = (R − D) / (R + D) of the two sides' contributions. The expected gap is a least-squares fit, pooled over every chamber, on every contested race with both sides' money on file: a per-year intercept + 30.8 points for the incumbent's side + 0.97 per point of presidential margin (16,170 races, R² 0.50). It uses no result, so it is refitted from whatever money is on file at each build (lib/stateLegMoneyModel.ts), and the backtest can fit it on every year without leakage. The same term at full-cycle receipts is taken out of each past seat row before it enters the lean, so a district's lean no longer carries one cycle's lopsided fundraising. Missing money on either side gives 0, the typical gap for the seat. 2026 receipts are to-date and scaled by 0.9, borrowed from the federal forecast. MONEY_K 0.03 and MONEY_CAP 1.5 are the best cell of a K × cap sweep on the 2022 and 2024 holdouts in both years (scripts/stateLegBacktest.ts --money); the gain falls as the cap grows, and a log-ratio basis and a forward-only term (no strip) did no better. Incumbency, seat noise and the chamber shock were re-measured with the term in. `npm run state-leg-money` rebuilds everything downstream when new fundraising lands.

The State TPL's State Legislature entry was the raw chamber aggregate — every counted district's votes summed — which counted a printed unopposed race as a 100–0 result and omitted an unprinted one, so it reported the margin of whichever seats happened to be contested and overstated the dominant party wherever unopposed races are printed. Each year now carries two rows that share the L weight on their turnout (lib/stateLegChamberMargin.ts): the contested pool (districts where both major parties drew votes, their own margin and votes) and the unopposed pool, imputed district by district from the presidential vote on the map the race was run on (data/stateLegPresByEra.ts, nearest presidential year), weighted by that presidential vote scaled to the chamber's legislative-to-presidential turnout ratio and stripping the environment of the source presidential year — the rule imputed House seats already followed. IMPUTED_LEG_ROW_WEIGHT = 1 (0.5 tested, no better). The environment fit is unchanged in form: imputed rows never enter it, so it now sees the contested pool. TPL_RAW_LEG=1 restores the old row for comparison.

EFFECT 2024 holdout: President 2.46 → 2.24, Senate 5.02 → 4.90, House average 3.06 → 3.41 (the harness references were re-baselined and the validation table re-emitted). 44 of 50 state TPLs move, 24 by half a point or more, mean 0.75, 34 toward Democrats: the states that print unopposed races compress toward their presidential lean — Wyoming R+44.7 → R+42.1, South Dakota R+30.8 → R+28.4, North Dakota R+35.0 → R+33.1, West Virginia R+36.2 → R+34.4, and on the other side Rhode Island D+20.3 → D+17.9 and Massachusetts D+30.0 → D+27.9.

Legislative District Atlas: presidential results on every map era, named candidates, redistricting history

Three datasets behind the new per-chamber district pages (/states/[state]/legislature/[chamber]). (A) 2016, 2020 and 2024 presidential votes inside every state legislative district on the current map and on each superseded map, from Dave's Redistricting 2020-VTD returns area-apportioned onto each era's polygons; the 2024-on-current cell stays the precinct-exact one. (B) Candidate names for every general election 2016–2025: Klarner's candidate file (names, incumbency, winners) for 2016–2022, MEDSL and the state canvasses for 2024, Wikipedia and the Secretaries of State for the odd years; winners, incumbents and unopposed flags derived where a source carried none. (C) Per-district redistricting history from population-weighted overlays of consecutive maps, with generated descriptions and the old-to-new crosswalk the 2026 projection will use.

EFFECT 99 chamber pages. 28,771 of 28,807 district-years named (99.9%). Overlay error on the 2024 holdout: median 0.04 points of margin across 73 chambers, worst Montana House 2.5. No model output changed.

New Hampshire 2022 House votes corrected from the state's election-database export

The county-PDF parse behind data-entry/new_hampshire_2022_legislature.csv had double-counted 17 districts whose candidate header spanned two groups, tripled Hillsborough 16, mis-assigned Rockingham 13 and 16 and Strafford 15, and omitted Strafford 16. EFFECT Chamber total 2,275,330 → 2,089,636; Democratic 1,154,052 → 1,044,611, Republican 1,102,958 → 1,033,731; margin D+2.2 → D+0.5. New Hampshire's State TPL moves with its 2022 State Legislature row (5% race-type weight).
