FORECAST · HOUSE, SENATE, GOVERNOR
How a race forecast is built

Every one of the 506 House, Senate and governor races on the ballot goes through the same recipe. We start with how the seat usually votes, adjust for this year’s conditions and candidates, and then check that against the polls. What comes out is a projected margin, a chance of winning and a rating.

STEP ONE
Five ingredients

The model's estimate adds up five things. Each is measured the same way for every race, so two races differ only because their seats, years and candidates differ.

1
How the seat usually votes
We call this the seat’s True Partisan Lean: what a typical Republican would get against a typical Democrat in a year that favored neither party, measured from every election there since 2016. Senate and governor races use the state’s lean; House races use the district’s, on the lines in place for 2026.
How lean is measured →
2
The national mood
The generic ballot asks voters which party they want in Congress. We turn its average into a national environment (now D+4.7) and apply it to each race, more strongly in states that have swung hard with the country before and less in states that have not. Generic-ballot polls have missed in the past, so the forecast allows for that rather than taking them at face value. How the generic ballot becomes a popular vote →

3
Incumbency
A sitting member running again gets a boost toward their party. The Senate and governor values are measured from past races; the House value was set by testing. Someone who was appointed to the seat and has never won it gets no boost: appointees have historically done no better than a newcomer.

4
Fundraising
Incumbents, and candidates in friendly territory, nearly always raise more, and the first three ingredients already account for that. So only the part of a fundraising gap that is unusual for a race like this one counts, and it is capped so a self-funder cannot buy a large shift.

5
The candidates
Some politicians reliably run ahead of their party, and some reliably run behind. If a nominee has been on a general-election ballot since 2016, how far they beat or trailed expectations carries forward, with older races counting less. A first-time candidate is treated as an average nominee.

STEP TWO
Then the polls

Where a race has been polled, the polling average is a second, independent estimate of the same margin. The more fresh polls a race has, the more the forecast leans on them. But the model always keeps at least 25% of the say, because in recent elections the polls have tended to miss in the same direction everywhere at once.

Which polls count?
General-election polls that name both nominees, fielded this year. Partisan polls are included and labeled, because leaving them out made the forecast worse.
How is the average made?
Each pollster counts once, with its most recent poll. Newer polls and bigger samples count more, and older polls are adjusted for how the national mood has moved since they were taken.
What about pollsters that lean one way?
If a pollster has consistently shown one party doing better than other pollsters in the same races this year, its numbers are adjusted by that amount. Grades for past accuracy are shown on the pollster ratings but are not used as weights, because a pollster’s accuracy in one cycle has not predicted its accuracy in the next.
Are polls taken at face value?
Not quite. In every recent election the final polls, taken together, have shown Democrats doing a little better than they did on election day: about 3 points in Senate races and 2 in governor races since 2016, and the national House vote has come in about 2.5 points more Republican than the generic ballot every time. The forecast reads each race’s polling average half that distance toward the Republican, the same hedge it applies to the generic ballot. The other half is treated as uncertainty, because the size of the miss has varied and was small in the last two midterms. The record, state by state →
What about undecided voters?
Up to 10 points of undecided voters in a poll are divided between the two candidates in proportion to their support. The leader stays the leader, and no assumption is made about which way undecided voters break.
STEP THREE
From a margin to a chance of winning

No forecast is exact. We measure how far off this method has been in past elections and use that to turn a projected margin into a probability. Part of the error is national: when the polls or the model are off, they are usually off in the same direction everywhere. House seats in the same state also tend to miss together, and the seat counts allow for that. The rest is specific to each race. A race with good polling has less of its own uncertainty than one without.

STEP FOUR
Who controls the House and the Senate

The chances for individual races can’t simply be added up, because races tend to miss together. Instead, the whole election is simulated 5,000 times. Each simulation draws one national surprise that hits every race at once, smaller shared swings among places with similar voters, and each race’s own luck. Counting seats across all the simulations gives the range and the odds of control.

Senate control needs 51 Democratic seats, because the Republican vice president breaks a 50–50 tie. Independents who caucus with Democrats are counted with them.
SPECIAL CASES
Races that don't fit the mold
Only one party on the ballot
If a major party has no nominee, or both general-election candidates are from the same party (as in some California races), the seat is counted as decided.
Independents
An independent who stands in for a party, such as one who caucuses with Democrats, is treated as that party's candidate. An independent running where one party has stepped aside, as in Nebraska's Senate race, is forecast on the seat's lean, their own record and their polls.
Three-way races
In Montana's Senate race the independent Seth Bodnar, not the Democratic nominee, is Kurt Alme's main opponent, so the forecast is Alme against Bodnar, and Democrat Alani Bankhead is a third candidate. Polls of three-way races have missed by about a third more than usual, so the forecast's uncertainty is wider here. Like Nebraska's Dan Osborn, a Bodnar win is counted on the Democratic side of the seat totals.
Louisiana's House races
All six seats are decided in an all-party primary on November 3, with a December runoff if needed. The forecast is for the combined Republican vote against the combined Democratic vote.
Alaska
Alaska ranks its votes, so only the final head-to-head round of a ranked-choice poll is used.

HONEST CAVEATS
What the forecast can't see
House races have leaned Republican
In 2022 and 2024, House results ran more Republican than the model expected. The forecast does not shift its estimates to correct for this, because a pattern from two elections is not a rule, but it allows for misses of that size in its ranges.
Governors are the hardest
Governors often build personal brands that cross party lines, which is why polls take over a governor's forecast fastest. Fundraising records are also missing for some governor races.
Records start in 2016
Long-serving incumbents' earlier over-performance doesn't count toward their candidate record.
Expert ratings aren't used
The forecast is built only from results, polls, money and candidates. You can see it alongside other forecasters on the forecast comparison.
